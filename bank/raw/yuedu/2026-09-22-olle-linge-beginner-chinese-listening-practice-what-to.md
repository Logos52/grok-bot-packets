Learning to understand spoken Chinese is both hard and easy at the same time. It’s hard because it takes time, but it’s also easy because the solution is not complicated.
To put this into concrete terms: engage in hundreds or thousands of hours of comprehensible input, and you’ll reach your goal. Listening that much is difficult in the same way that walking a thousand miles is difficult; not because each step is hard or that walking is complicated, but because there are so many steps in total.
If you’ve found this article as a beginner, you’re already asking the right question. Many students assume that going to class, working through a textbook and listening to the accompanying audio will somehow provide enough listening.
For learners who want broad proficiency in Mandarin, listening is the most important skill of all. Understanding people is essential for communication, and listening also gives you the words, grammar, sounds, tones and patterns you later use yourself. It is also much easier to fit large amounts of listening into everyday life than it is to fit in comparable amounts of speaking, reading or writing.
The basic advice is simple: listen to as much Chinese as possible, while understanding as much as possible. This article is about how to do that as a beginner, even if you know no Chinese at all, and which resources you can use.

You learn the most from listening when you can make sense of what you listen to. When you understand, you not only get better at processing the words you hear, but you also stand a better chance of learning more about how those words are used, grammar, and even unfamiliar words.
This is the basic idea behind comprehensible input: engage with as much spoken Mandarin as you can, while making sure you understand most of it.
Learning Chinese through comprehensible input
You don’t need to understand every word, and you certainly don’t need to be able to explain all the grammar. What matters is that you understand enough of what is being communicated for the Chinese to mean something to you.
When that happens, you build connections between spoken forms (sounds) and meaning. Common words become easier and quicker to recognise, grammar becomes familiar in context, and the sounds and tones of Mandarin stop sounding like gibberish.
This is one reason listening is so important. It does more than train the narrow skill of answering listening-comprehension questions. It gives you a huge amount of Chinese in context and builds the mental model you need for spoken Mandarin. Speaking well is what most students say they are after, but listening is in fact much more important than speaking, and will also result in better speaking.
Listening mostly to Mandarin you can understand sounds reasonable, but there’s a problem: How do you do that when you’re a beginner?
The answer is that meaning isn’t only conveyed through words. A skilled teacher can use objects, pictures, drawings, gestures, facial expressions, repetition and the situation itself to make simple Chinese understandable without translating into English. For this to work, you need video, not just audio.
It’s important to note the difference between a zero beginner, who knows no Chinese or almost none, and a beginner who knows at least some basic words and phrases. Once you have a small foundation, pure listening becomes feasible, but when you know little or nothing, visual support is necessary.

Video is not the only way to make Chinese comprehensible for zero beginners. A good teacher or tutor can tailor what they say to you specifically, and if you prepare before you listen, you can also tackle beginner content which isn’t designed specifically for zero beginners.
