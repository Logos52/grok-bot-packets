### Arguments
- 02:05 · Robert Tombs · **Claim:** England/France (and neighbors) got their national identities through fighting each other — the rivalry is still live because both were world-historical powers that treat each other as opposites (while being rather similar) and as scorekeepers of mutual decline. **Support:** 19th-c French historian on Hundred Years War; Scots–France alliance vs England–France conflict; post-Waterloo French decline talk mirrored by British “nothing works”; Le Monde/Times mutual catastrophe tourism; Brexit re-injected rivalry. **Rejects:** rivalry as mere ancient folklore; identity as prior to conflict; one-sided British or French exceptionalism as the whole story.
- 04:38 / 05:44 · Robert Tombs · **Claim:** European integration is France’s great national project — Britain walking away was a slap in the face, so France needs Brexit to fail (or look like a failure) to protect the EU’s legitimacy. **Support:** French proposals since 1920s; establishment consensus that EU is in France’s interest; if Brexit succeeds or is merely non-disastrous it reflects badly on the whole project. **Rejects:** EU as neutral technocratic peace machine equally owned by all members; French Brexit hostility as mere trade/pique without project-preservation stakes.
- 05:44 · Robert Tombs · **Claim:** From the French elite view the EU exists first to neutralize Germany and second to restore French leadership of Europe — great-power theater after real great-power status is gone. **Support:** Germany invaded France three times in ~150 years; friendship gestures with Germany as mandatory (like UK–US special relationship theater); de Gaulle: behave as a great power because we no longer are; leading Europe since 1950s, nothing allowed to obstruct it. **Rejects:** exclusive UK–France rivalry as the only dyad (France–Germany is the French analogue of UK–US); EU as primarily German or American design.
- 07:56 · Konstantin / Robert Tombs · **Claim (Konstantin):** Britain’s standing interest is blocking a continental hegemon that France repeatedly sought — Brexit is the latest iteration. **Claim (Tombs):** Same post-imperial challenge, opposite national responses — France remakes greatness by making Europe; Brexit Britain by leaving the bloc. **Support:** Suez failure → French “never trust America / look to Europe” vs British “never quarrel with America”; reluctant British Common Market entry vs French federal great-power aim with Paris as capital; US wanted UK inside as pro-American check (French: Trojan horse/poodle). **Rejects:** Brexit as sui generis cultural spasm; British EU membership as ever having been the center of UK foreign policy the way it was for France.
- 11:22 · Robert Tombs · **Claim:** Napoleon’s Europe — single law/admin, sweep old distinctions, abolish feudalism/church power, curb antisemitism — is recognizably akin to later European-integration modernizing visions; Britain refused both his hegemony and his model. **Support:** Napoleon’s “not enough sameness” remark; bureaucratic-military empire; British determination not to live under Napoleonic-dominated Europe; ~15–20 years of global war that set the power map for a century. **Rejects:** Napoleon as only battlefield romantic; EU as wholly discontinuous with older French hegemonic modernization projects.
- 13:08 · Robert Tombs · **Claim:** The French Revolution was essentially a reaction to military/fiscal failure — recreating France as a power — not an Enlightenment seminar that decided to modernize. **Support:** Loss of Seven Years War; American independence win that produced vast debts and neutered France for lack of money; Burke (as Tombs recalls): not to make France happy but powerful; Robespierre/Danton as rights-talk plus European French influence; Napoleon “cleaned the mud” and gave permanent institutions without dropping dominance ambition. **Rejects:** textbook arc Enlightenment → democracy → Bastille as primary cause; revolution as mainly peasant/worker social justice story.
- 16:05 · Robert Tombs · **Claim:** Britain went to war with revolutionary France over the Low Countries invasion threat (permanent anti-enemy-in-Flanders/Netherlands policy), not because British opinion rejected revolutionary ideas. **Support:** Many Whigs / Charles James Fox liked the Revolution; cricket-tour anecdote to “calm” France after Bastille; Thames-estuary invasion geography — Toulon/Brest navies distant, Ostend/Rotterdam intolerable; Ireland as potential Spanish/French/German jumping-off point (de Valera’s condolences to German legation cited as symbolic exception). **Rejects:** Britain-as-anti-Jacobin crusade as the casus belli; Ireland policy as primarily cultural rather than strategic denial.
- 20:05 · Robert Tombs · **Claim:** Revolutions follow a patent — breakdown, factional chaos, then authoritarian restoration that most people accept as better than killing/inflation — and Napoleon was France’s version (Lenin/Stalin Russia’s). **Support:** Bastille / Winter Palace collapse → “everyone wants a piece” → “restore order”; end state more powerful/oppressive than the old regime; public relief after Terror/chaos. **Rejects:** revolution as durable liberation endpoint; Napoleon as pure betrayal rather than order-demand fulfillment.
- 24:06 · Robert Tombs · **Claim:** Franco-British “radically different political DNA” is overstated — French 19th-c historians saw England’s Charles I → Cromwell → restoration as the template France was replaying, and French monarchy persisted (multiple restorations + Napoleon III) until 1870 defeat. **Support:** Louis XVI / Robespierre / Napoleon / Louis XVIII / Louis-Philippe / Napoleon III sequence; monarchy to 1870; German invasion as the real terminator; Britain unconquered since 1066 as stability reason; 1649 as semi-revolution/religious war most people didn’t want (Levellers later to Charles II). **Rejects:** 1793 as clean permanent end of French monarchy; British continuity as proof the French path was uniquely ideological rather than defeat-driven.

### Closing
Robert Tombs walks Konstantin and Francis through a thousand-year rivalry that is less cartoon enmity than co-produced identity: England and France became themselves by fighting, still score each other’s decline in the papers, and still answer the same post-imperial question in opposite ways — France by making Europe its national project (lock Germany, lead the continent, survive as a great power in form), Brexit Britain by walking away so as not to be a mere bloc province, which is why Paris needs Brexit to look like a disaster. The middle of the show dismantles exportable myths: the Revolution as Enlightenment seminar (Tombs: defeat, debt, Burkean power-restoration); Napoleon as French national romantic (Corsican user of France, EU-ish sameness engineer, undone by overreach, a refused Channel crossing, and a Spanish ulcer as deadly as Russia); 1940 as cheese-eating surrender (they fought, bled more than Britain, and lost a war everyone had prepared as 1914-redux while German armor punched the “impossible” Ardennes). Vichy emerges as counterrevolutionary patriotism under Pétain as much as fascism, the Resistance as 1944 logistics plus de Gaulle’s legitimacy machine more than a war-winning army, and Dunkirk/Brexit as rhyming French stories of British abandonment. The present tense is the sting: Le Pen’s ceiling is still partly Vichy memory, Mélenchon is the nightmare other pole, and Tombs’s fear is not that France and Britain remain opposites but that both are sliding into the same polarized, center-hollow, weak-government future — history’s rivals converging on a shared incapacity to govern.
