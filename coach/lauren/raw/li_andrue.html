Lauren's Guide to Supervising Coding Agents with pstack

This title was summarized by AI from the post below.
Andrue Anderson

The hard part of agent coding is not prompting harder. It is supervising someone smarter than you. Lauren (@poteto) published "The Complete Guide to pstack Pt. 2" on how she does research, planning, prototyping, and architecture with pstack. She frames two failure modes she keeps seeing: agents misunderstand intent because the ask is underspecified, or they lack enough context to do the work correctly. Her fix is priming context, not micromanaging every line. One technique she likes: the indirect prompt. Ask the agent to restate a Slack thread in its own words before writing code, so you catch the wrong problem early without planting your own hypothesis. Skills like /teach (backed by /how for runtime mechanics and /why for historical intent across git, tickets, docs, Slack, monitors), plus /recall for prior chat context, are how she keeps a shared mental model when no human fits the whole codebase in their head anymore. She is skeptical of abstract plan modes. Prefer planning with code: README-driven targets shaped with a Diátaxis-aware /technical-writing skill, prototyping playbooks that build throwaway variants and verify them, and /architect that grounds the problem, sketches competing designs across model families, cross-judges, then implements or scraps when types need escape hatches. Treat her "thousands of PRs a month" claim as hers. If plan mode only produces a long checklist you never run, you do not have a plan. You have a comfort document. If you are designing the supervision layer for coding agents on a product team, DM me about AI agents and product strategy. https://lnkd.in/enfwQ9uS https://lnkd.in/eZW3EuVQ

    I killed a working system because the code was ugly. That was the most expensive decision I made as a CTO. At Tatango we had a system long in the tooth. Patched, inconsistent, genuinely badly designed in places. I argued hard for a full rewrite instead of building on the bones of what was there. I won that argument. Here's what I didn't understand. The code was bad because it had been patched. And every one of those patches was a lesson — a production incident somebody had already survived, encoded as an ugly conditional nobody had time to document. That wasn't technical debt. That was institutional knowledge wearing a bad costume. We rewrote it clean. Then we learned every one of those lessons a second time, in production, against a schedule we'd already committed to. I'd defend this position now: don't rewrite unless it's genuinely necessary. And if you do, the ugly patches are your requirements document. Read them before you delete them. A rewrite isn't expensive because of the engineering hours. It's expensive because you're throwing away the only surviving record of what already went wrong. What's the ugliest code in your system — and do you actually know why it's shaped that way?
