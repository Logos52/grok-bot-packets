The CEO of McDonald's told investors flatly that traffic from low-income households was still falling by nearly double digits while visits from wealthier customers were climbing by nearly double digits at the very same time. He pointed to high rents, high food prices, and high child care costs grinding the low-income consumer down. Economists have a name for this now. They call it the K-shaped economy. The line on the chart splits in two. People near the top keep climbing, spending freely. People near the bottom slide the other way, cutting back, getting squeezed out. And fast food, of all things, has become one of the clearest places you can actually watch that split happen in real time. In early 2026, you could see it inside a single afternoon of earnings reports. The value brand Taco Bell posted blockbuster sales. Another chain, the kind favored by stretched customers, cratering on the same day. The same drive-thru, two completely different Americans pulling up to the window. For its entire history, fast food was a classless place. The same McDonald's served the senator and the janitor, the executive and the kid working the register. There was something almost democratic about it, exactly like that nickel hamburger promised back in 1921. That is the thing now quietly coming apart. More than 60% of lower-income Americans now say fast food is a luxury they can no longer afford. The food built for people with no money is slowly turning into food for people who have some. Globally, the fast food business is still growing, projected to climb from around $800 billion today to well over a trillion within the next decade. But almost all of that growth is coming from developing countries. Here at home, in the market that invented it, the volume is flattening because the people it was built for are being pushed out. So why [music] did fast food become so unaffordable? The most honest answer doesn't really fit into a bumper sticker. There was no single villain. There was a government that printed and spent money on a scale never seen before, creating the inflation that everything else grew out of. There was a pandemic and a war that shattered supply chains. There was a cattle herd at a 75-year low. There was a labor market that got rebuilt from the ground up. There were delivery apps skimming a cut off the top, [music] and some big brands flexing their muscles to grab a little extra margin, and rent stacked on [music] rent, and portions quietly shrinking on the tray. It was economics, not ideology. A hundred different pressures all shoving in the same direction on the same little paper bag. And this is the part worth holding on to. The working class built fast food. Factory workers and night shift laborers and people counting their change that made the nickel hamburger a national institution. And now, the very same forces that have squeezed their wages and inflated their rent and stretched every dollar they earn are slowly pricing them out of meal that was invented from the very beginning just for them.
